# Role Visual Identity System
## UmojaHub Storyworld Character Design Specification

**Design Law:** Every role must be identifiable from its silhouette alone within 2 seconds. If a label is required for recognition, the design has failed.

---

## 01 — Farmer

### Recognition Objective
A visitor who has never heard of UmojaHub must immediately understand: this person grows food. Not a generic laborer. Not a rural person. Someone who works the land and produces crops for sale.

### Silhouette Design
Wide-brimmed field hat creates a distinctive crown profile. Gumboots (Wellington boots) widen the lower silhouette and ground the figure. A harvest basket — kiondo — carried at the hip or in one hand breaks the outline asymmetrically. The combination of hat brim + gumboots + basket produces a silhouette no other role shares.

Secondary option: a filled maize sack over one shoulder creates a strong diagonal load that reads immediately as harvest weight.

### Accessories
- **Kiondo basket** — traditional woven Kenyan harvest basket, either carried or set beside the figure. Primary recognition cue.
- **Gumboots** — tall rubber Wellington boots, universally associated with East African farm labor. Mandatory for silhouette differentiation.
- **Jembe (hoe) or machete** — leaned against the figure or held loosely. Do not render as a weapon silhouette — keep it agricultural.
- **Wide-brimmed field hat** — straw or canvas, worn, slightly misshapen from use.
- **Secondary: maize sack** — burlap or woven polypropylene, tied at top. Only if primary basket is absent.

### Clothing Direction
Work shirt — loose-fitting cotton, possibly a kikoy wrap at the waist over trousers. Not pristine. Worn colors: earth tones, faded greens, dusty ochre. Trousers rolled slightly above gumboots. The clothing should look like it has been worn while working, not styled for a photoshoot.

Avoid: matching sets, clean white shirts, formal footwear.

### Posture Language
Grounded. Weight distributed evenly across both feet. Slight forward tilt of the torso — the posture of someone accustomed to bending and lifting. When standing at rest, one hand may rest on the basket or tool. Not a proud, chest-out stance — a practical, experienced stance. The body communicates physical knowledge of the land.

### Motion Behavior
Enters from far off-camera with a deliberate, unhurried gait. Carries slight weight in the step — not heavy-footed, but grounded. The basket or sack should have subtle physics — slight swing on movement. When speaking: minimal gesture, weight shifts between feet occasionally, hand may move to resting on tool.

### Environmental Cues
A small scatter of produce around the figure's feet — three or four low-poly tomatoes, maize cobs, or a bundle of greens. Not a market stall. Just what a farmer would set down while talking.

### Cultural References
- Kikuyu and Kalenjin smallholder practices in the Central Highlands and Rift Valley
- Luo and Luhya farming communities in Western Kenya
- The kiondo as everyday working vessel, not tourist artifact
- Gumboots as the universal East African farm worker footwear — they appear in Kenyan hip-hop imagery, political cartoons, and news photography
- Research reference: Kenya Agricultural and Livestock Research Organization farmer documentation; FAO Kenya smallholder profiles

### Recognition Test
Convert the character to a flat black silhouette. Show it for 2 seconds. Ask: "What does this person do?" The correct answer must be "farmer" or "grows food." If the answer is "worker" or "person" without agricultural specificity, redesign.

### Failure Modes
- Gumboots omitted → loses primary ground-level recognition cue
- Hat omitted → head shape merges with other characters
- Basket replaced with vague object → role becomes "laborer," not "farmer"
- Clothing too clean → reads as performance of farming, not farming
- Upright confident posture → loses the grounded agricultural quality

## 02 — Buyer

### Recognition Objective
A visitor must immediately understand: this person evaluates and purchases. Not a manager. Not an office worker. Someone whose job is to inspect, assess, and decide whether to buy.

### Silhouette Design
A clipboard held upright at chest height creates an immediately recognizable vertical rectangle extending above the hand. This is the primary recognition element. The buyer's free hand is often extended or pointing — the gesture of inspection. Combined with a slightly more formal collar line than the farmer, the silhouette reads: evaluator.

### Accessories
- **Clipboard with order sheet** — primary recognition cue. Should be visible from the side, not tucked under the arm. Held actively.
- **Pen** — held or clipped to clipboard. Secondary detail.
- **Phone** — in breast pocket or hand when clipboard is at rest. A modern buyer cross-references prices digitally.
- **Tote or shoulder bag** — practical, not fashion. Holds samples.

### Clothing Direction
Smart casual. Collared shirt, possibly a lightweight vest or waistcoat over it. Well-fitted trousers. Clean shoes — not formal shoes, but maintained footwear that distinguishes from field workers. Colors: muted blues, khaki, grey-green. The clothing says: professional, but not corporate.

Avoid: suits, ties, branded polos, sports casual.

### Posture Language
Upright and slightly critical. Weight on one foot, the other stepped slightly forward in an evaluative stance. Head tilted marginally — the posture of someone reading a list and cross-referencing what they see. One hand active (clipboard), one hand potentially touching chin or crossed loosely at waist.

### Motion Behavior
Enters with purpose and efficiency — this person is on a schedule. Slightly quicker arrival than the farmer. When speaking: uses the clipboard hand occasionally as a gesture aid. Nods deliberately when receiving information. Does not shift weight as much as the farmer — more controlled.

### Environmental Cues
A low stack of sample crates or produce beside the figure — but not the farmer's harvest volume. The buyer has already selected items to inspect, not a full harvest. A small scale or measuring tool nearby reinforces the evaluation role.

### Cultural References
- Nairobi wholesale market procurement officers at Wakulima Market
- Hotel and restaurant purchasing managers in Mombasa and Nairobi
- Export aggregators and produce traders at border crossings
- Research reference: Kenya National Bureau of Statistics agri-trade data; market buyer documentation from Twiga Foods, Copia Kenya sourcing models

### Recognition Test
Silhouette test: the clipboard must be visible and prominent. The posture must read "evaluator" not "walker." 2 seconds, no label: answer must be "buyer" or "someone checking/buying."

### Failure Modes
- Clipboard absent → becomes indistinguishable from employer or institution rep
- Clipboard tucked under arm → loses primary recognition cue
- Posture too relaxed → reads as bystander
- Suit-level formality → reads as manager, loses the procurement-specific identity

## 03 — Student

### Recognition Objective
A visitor must immediately understand: this person is learning and building credentials. Not a young person. Not an entry-level worker. A student actively engaged in academic and project work.

### Silhouette Design
A backpack is the non-negotiable element. It creates a distinct protrusion from the rear upper silhouette that no other role shares. The student should carry or hold a notebook or tablet as a secondary cue. The combination of backpack + notebook in hand creates an unmistakable silhouette.

### Accessories
- **Backpack** — mandatory. Must be visible in profile. Medium size — a school/university carry bag, not a hiking pack.
- **Notebook or tablet** — held open or under one arm. If notebook, it should be open to a visible page.
- **Earphones/earbud** — worn around neck or in one ear. Signals digital engagement.
- **Water bottle** — clipped to backpack side. Common university student cue.

### Clothing Direction
Casual academic. T-shirt or light cotton shirt. Jeans or casual trousers. Clean sneakers. Colors: mid-toned, contemporary — the student has a personal style even if modest. A university hoodie or sweatshirt is appropriate and strongly recognizable. Do not over-age the clothing — this character should read young.

Avoid: formal professional attire, ties, worn work clothing.

### Posture Language
Alert and engaged. Weight forward slightly — eager posture, the posture of someone who is interested and hasn't yet developed the skeptical guard of established professionals. Head slightly raised when receiving information. When using the tablet or notebook, head angled down with focus. The student moves quickly between focused and listening modes.

### Motion Behavior
Arrives with energy — quicker step than others. May stop and take a note at key moments. When speaking: gestures with the free hand more than other characters. Posture shifts between attentive upright and lean-in engagement.

### Environmental Cues
A floating portfolio card at arm's length during the demo sequence — this is what replaces the particle trail for the student's episode. A low-poly screen or document artifact near the character.

### Cultural References
- University of Nairobi, Kenyatta University, Strathmore tech students
- USIU-Africa and KCA University student demographics
- The backpack is universal; the tablet/notebook combination reflects actual East African university digital-physical workflows
- Research reference: Kenya Universities and Colleges Central Placement Service student demographics; Strathmore iLabAfrica student profiles

### Recognition Test
Remove the label. Show for 2 seconds. Answer must be "student" or "young person studying." If backpack is absent, retest — it may fail without it.

### Failure Modes
- Backpack absent → becomes indistinguishable from employer or young buyer
- Notebook absent → loses the learning-in-progress signal
- Clothing too formal → reads as young professional, not student
- Posture too reserved → loses the eager, building quality
- Laptop "floating" as abstract prop (not held or worn) → loses authenticity

## 04 — Lecturer

### Recognition Objective
A visitor must immediately understand: this person reviews and validates. Not an older student. Not a bureaucrat. Someone with academic authority who evaluates work and gives credentialed assessments.

### Silhouette Design
The key differentiator from the student: the lecturer holds documents that belong to someone else. A review portfolio, a printed assessment, papers with annotations — these are not the lecturer's own work but someone else's, being evaluated. Reading glasses (worn or held) create a specific silhouette detail no other character has. A slightly more formal garment (blazer or academic robe element) broadens the shoulder silhouette relative to the student.

### Accessories
- **Review portfolio or assessment papers** — held openly, with visible markings. Primary recognition cue.
- **Reading glasses** — worn on face or held in one hand. Strongly signals academic evaluator.
- **Pen** — active annotation tool, not a clipboard pen.
- **Briefcase or document folder** — at feet or under arm. More formal than student backpack.

### Clothing Direction
Smart academic. A blazer over a shirt — not necessarily a suit jacket, but something with structure. Button-front shirt. Well-maintained trousers. Leather shoes or formal flats. Colors: deep blues, charcoal, burgundy, olive — serious colors. The clothing should have visible texture and weight compared to the student's casual layers.

Avoid: the student's casual register; ultra-formal academic gowns (reads as ceremonial, not working); corporate business attire.

### Posture Language
Considered and deliberate. The stance of someone who has formed opinions and is weighing how to express them. Weight even, feet planted. Head slightly down when reviewing, then raised for direct eye contact when speaking. Not stiff — experienced ease. The posture of someone who has stood in front of lecture halls and knows how to hold space.

### Motion Behavior
Arrives with measured pace. Does not rush. Pauses before speaking — the rhythm of a lecturer who allows silence. When reviewing the portfolio: small page-turn gestures, a moment of reading-body-language (head angled down, eyes moving). When responding: slightly raised chin.

### Environmental Cues
A low-poly lectern or desk element nearby. Stacked papers or a document tray. A subtle blackboard or projection surface in the far background.

### Cultural References
- University of Nairobi Faculty of Agriculture, Jomo Kenyatta University of Agriculture and Technology faculty
- The East African academic tradition of formal-but-accessible academic dress
- Research reference: JKUAT and UoN lecturer profiles; Kenya National Qualifications Authority documentation on lecturer credentialing

### Recognition Test
Silhouette must be clearly distinct from student. The reading glasses + review papers combination must read as "evaluator" not "user." 2 seconds: answer must be "professor/lecturer" or "person reviewing/assessing."

### Failure Modes
- No reading glasses → silhouette too similar to buyer or employer
- Papers look like own work rather than someone else's assessment → reads as student
- Casual clothing → merges with student visually
- Rushed posture → loses academic authority

## 05 — Employer

### Recognition Objective
A visitor must immediately understand: this person decides who gets hired. Not a manager doing general management. Not a buyer. Someone specifically in the act of evaluating candidates and making opportunity decisions.

### Silhouette Design
The employer holds a candidate file or CV folder — not a procurement clipboard. This is the key distinction from the buyer. The file should be slightly thinner and more formal than the buyer's clipboard. The employer's posture is more upright and considered — evaluating potential, not checking inventory. A lanyard with ID badge is an extremely strong recognition cue for this role.

### Accessories
- **CV/candidate folder** — open or closed, held at chest height. Slightly formal document presentation.
- **Lanyard with ID badge** — worn. This single element strongly communicates "organizational professional with institutional affiliation."
- **Pen or stylus** — active note-taking on candidates.
- **Phone** — in hand or pocket, for cross-referencing digital portfolio.

### Clothing Direction
Professional business casual. A collared shirt or blouse, well-fitted. Trousers with a distinct cut. A light blazer is appropriate. Smart shoes. Colors: navy, charcoal, burgundy, sage — organizational colors. The employer should look like they belong to an institution without being a bureaucrat.

Avoid: suits with ties (too corporate-formal); casual registers (loses hiring authority).

### Posture Language
Upright and assessing. The posture of someone who interviews people regularly — slightly formal, composed, giving nothing away in the face while maintaining open body language. Arms not crossed (too closed) but not wide open (too casual). The file is held in front as a reference point.

### Motion Behavior
Arrives with professional confidence — not rushed, not hesitant. When speaking: direct eye contact, measured gestures. When reviewing the portfolio entry: deliberate attention, a slight lean forward. The employer is used to making decisions and the body shows it.

### Environmental Cues
A subtle hiring board or job posting behind the figure. A small table with candidate materials. The environment suggests a review setting, not a field or office — a neutral evaluation space.

### Cultural References
- Nairobi tech sector hiring managers (iHub, Andela alumni, Safaricom talent acquisition)
- East African agribusiness HR professionals
- Research reference: Andela hiring process documentation; Kenya Private Sector Alliance employer profiles

### Recognition Test
Must be distinguishable from buyer (who also holds documents) by posture and the nature of the document. Lanyard should be visible. 2 seconds: answer must be "hiring manager" or "someone evaluating candidates/hiring."

### Failure Modes
- Clipboard (buyer prop) instead of candidate folder → merges with buyer role
- No lanyard → loses institutional affiliation signal
- Too stiff/formal → reads as government official
- Too casual → reads as colleague, not evaluator

## 06 — NGO Representative

### Recognition Objective
A visitor must immediately understand: this person works in community programs and impact evaluation. Not a government official. Not a corporate professional. Someone who operates between organizations and communities in field-facing roles.

### Silhouette Design
The field vest is the primary recognition cue. Multiple-pocket vests are universally associated with NGO and field program work — journalists, researchers, and NGO field staff wear them globally. The vest creates visible pocket detail on the torso that breaks the flat silhouette of other characters. A lanyard with field badge reinforces the organizational affiliation.

### Accessories
- **Field vest** — multi-pocket, lightweight. Mandatory primary recognition cue.
- **Field notebook or program binder** — held or clipped to vest. Contains program data, not commercial documents.
- **Lanyard with field badge** — different style from employer lanyard; more field-worn.
- **Reusable water bottle** — signals long field days.
- **Rolled document or map** — under the arm or in a vest pocket.

### Clothing Direction
Practical and field-appropriate over a light shirt or t-shirt. The vest is the signature. Below: cargo trousers or sturdy chinos. Field-appropriate footwear — trail runners or sturdy sandals. Colors: khaki, olive, teal, grey — field operational colors that differ from both agricultural earth tones and corporate professional tones.

Avoid: suits; pure casual; uniforms that read as military or government.

### Posture Language
Open and community-engaged. The posture of someone who spends time in communities and has learned to signal approachability — hands not in pockets, body not angled away, weight balanced and present. When reviewing documents: participatory lean, not evaluative authority. When speaking: inclusive gesture, draws the listener in.

### Motion Behavior
Arrives from a direction suggesting they come from the field — not from an office. Slight purposefulness but also ease. When speaking: references program materials with familiarity, flips through notebook with institutional knowledge.

### Environmental Cues
A subtle map or impact chart nearby. A small group of low-poly background figures suggesting community work context. The environment should feel like an intersection point — not a village, not an office.

### Cultural References
- Field staff from CARE Kenya, Oxfam Kenya, World Food Programme Kenya, Fairtrade Africa
- The field vest as the universal NGO field worker visual mark — visible in Kenya, Uganda, Tanzania, Ethiopia, Rwanda field program photography
- Research reference: CARE Kenya and WFP Kenya field operations; International Livestock Research Institute field staff documentation

### Recognition Test
Field vest must be the dominant silhouette element. 2 seconds: answer must be "NGO worker" or "field program person." Must be clearly distinct from the employer (institutional but not field) and the buyer (commercial).

### Failure Modes
- Field vest absent → merges with employer or buyer
- Corporate clothing → loses the community-field distinction
- Government-style badge or uniform → reads as official/bureaucratic
- Too casual without the vest → reads as generic bystander

## 07 — Cooperative Leader

### Recognition Objective
A visitor must immediately understand: this person represents and coordinates a group of farmers. Not an individual farmer. Not a buyer. Someone who speaks for many and has organizational responsibilities beyond personal production.

### Silhouette Design
The cooperative leader should be visually elevated relative to the individual farmer — slightly more formal clothing, an organizational vest or branded jacket, and a document organizer rather than a harvest basket. The posture is upright leadership posture rather than the farmer's grounded field posture. A visible badge or cooperative symbol on the garment reinforces the representative role.

### Accessories
- **Cooperative group vest or branded jacket** — the most important element. Many East African cooperatives issue branded vests to leadership. This single item separates the cooperative leader from the individual farmer.
- **Document organizer or group register** — holds records for 14 farmers, not one. Visible bulk suggests organizational scope.
- **Phone** — cooperative coordinators are constantly on call managing members.
- **Badge or cooperative insignia** — on vest or jacket.

### Clothing Direction
The cooperative vest over a clean shirt — the vest is the key differentiator. Below: formal trousers rather than work trousers. Polished but practical shoes. The cooperative leader has stepped up from farm work into coordination — the clothing reflects that transition without abandoning the agricultural community.

Avoid: the individual farmer's field attire (must visually separate); full business formal (loses community connection).

### Posture Language
Leadership posture — upright, shoulders back, but not stiff. The bearing of someone who represents others and carries that responsibility consciously. One hand may hold the document organizer; the other gestures in explanation — this person explains and coordinates regularly. Slightly wider stance than the individual farmer — the physical confidence of recognized organizational authority.

### Motion Behavior
Arrives with deliberate purpose — on behalf of others, not just themselves. When speaking: references the group register, specific numbers come up ("fourteen farmers"). Gestures are inclusive and wide — representing many, not just self.

### Environmental Cues
Subtle cluster of small background figures (low-poly) behind or around the cooperative leader — the farmers they represent, rendered minimally. A group coordination board or register stack nearby.

### Cultural References
- Dairy cooperatives: Kenya Cooperative Creameries structure; Githunguri Dairy Cooperative leadership
- Tea cooperatives: KTDA (Kenya Tea Development Agency) cooperative committee members
- Coffee cooperatives: Kenya Coffee Growers and Millers Association cooperative structure
- The SACCOS (Savings and Credit Co-operative Societies) cooperative leadership model
- Research reference: Co-operative Alliance of Kenya; KTDA annual reports showing cooperative leadership

### Recognition Test
Must be distinguishable from individual farmer (less field-worn, more formal, organizational items) and from buyer (group register vs. procurement clipboard, cooperative vest vs. smart casual). 2 seconds: answer must be "cooperative leader" or "someone representing a group."

### Failure Modes
- Wearing individual farmer clothing → merges completely with the farmer
- No organizational vest/badge → loses the representative role signal
- Upright posture identical to employer → loses agricultural community connection
- Group register absent → loses the "representing many" element

## 08 — Institution Representative

### Recognition Objective
A visitor must immediately understand: this person represents a formal institution — a university, government body, or regulatory authority — and comes with institutional accountability. Not a bureaucrat. Not a corporate professional. Someone whose word carries the weight of their institution.

### Silhouette Design
Formal blazer or structured jacket creates a more rigid silhouette than the NGO worker's vest. An official badge — not a lanyard, but a proper institutional ID — distinguishes from the employer. A document portfolio (closed, formal) rather than an open review file. The overall silhouette reads: official delegation.

### Accessories
- **Official ID badge or institutional badge** — higher formality than a lanyard; possibly a pin or mounted badge. Primary institutional signal.
- **Formal document portfolio** — closed. The institution representative doesn't casually open their materials; they present them.
- **Brief case or structured bag** — not a casual bag. Structured, maintained.
- **Pen** — quality pen, not a disposable one. Detail that reads in close view.

### Clothing Direction
Formal professional. Blazer and shirt/blouse. Well-tailored trousers or skirt. Formal shoes, maintained. Colors: institutional colors — deep navy, dark grey, formal burgundy, forest green. The clothing has structure and authority but is not a costume.

Avoid: overly bureaucratic (stiff, dated); ultra-corporate (loses public-institution quality); casual register.

### Posture Language
Composed and measured. The posture of institutional authority without arrogance. Standing straight, feet together or in a formal stance. The portfolio held in both hands or under one arm — a presentation posture. When speaking: careful articulation, measured pace, the weight of institutional responsibility visible in the deliberateness.

### Motion Behavior
Arrives with careful, formal entrance. Does not rush. When speaking: precise language visible in the deliberate gesture. When presenting the portfolio: a formal offering gesture — this document is official.

### Environmental Cues
A subtle institutional seal or crest behind the figure. A formal signing table or presentation surface nearby. The environment should feel like an official meeting point.

### Cultural References
- Kenya National Qualifications Authority representatives
- Universities and Colleges Central Placement Service officers
- East African Community institutional delegates
- Kenya Revenue Authority compliance officers (for the formal documentation culture)
- Research reference: KRA institutional documentation style; Kenya National Qualifications Authority officer profiles

### Recognition Test
Must be distinct from employer (higher formality, no candidate file), from lecturer (institutional rather than academic-individual), from NGO (formal structure vs. field engagement). 2 seconds: answer must be "official" or "institution representative."

### Failure Modes
- Too similar to employer (same blazer, same file) → roles merge
- Too bureaucratic/stiff → becomes a caricature of officialdom
- Badge absent → loses institutional affiliation signal
- Posture too casual → loses formal authority

## 09 — The Administrator (The Guide)

### Recognition Objective
The Administrator is the character who appears first, remains throughout all six episodes, and never leaves The Arch. A visitor must immediately feel: this person is trustworthy, knowledgeable, and genuinely invested in the outcome. Not an authority figure who judges. A steward who guides.

This character is the hardest to design and the most important.

### Silhouette Design
The Guide must be visually distinguishable from all other characters simultaneously — since they will be on screen while others arrive. The key design choices:
- Slightly taller or broader silhouette than the participants — not imposing, but visibly the established presence
- A garment with visible structure that reads as belonging to this place — The Arch is their home
- Hands that are often open, gesturing toward The Arch or toward the participant — an inclusive, explanatory posture that is constant
- No heavy accessories — this character's weight comes from presence, not objects

### Accessories
- **Minimal** — The Administrator holds nothing heavy. A small tablet or folded note card at most — referencing the system's data when specific facts are cited.
- **A subtle garment marker** — not a logo, not a badge, but something that reads as belonging to the platform: a pin, a woven element, a specific color accent in the clothing.
- **Open hands** — the primary "accessory" is the gesture of availability. Palms forward, palms out — the posture of the person who has nothing to hide.

### Clothing Direction
Contemporary and considered. Not corporate. Not casual. A clean, well-fitted shirt or structured top in a neutral that complements the teal of the platform — a deep slate blue, a charcoal, a warm dark grey. Functional trousers. Clean footwear. The clothing should age well in the viewer's memory — it should not be trend-specific.

The garment should have one deliberate color element in the platform's teal — a collar detail, a wrist element, a subtle accent — that links this character to The Arch without being a uniform.

Avoid: suits (too corporate); robes (too spiritual); tech company t-shirts (too casual-brand); medical white (too clinical); mascot-adjacent anything.

### Posture Language
The most important posture in the system. The Guide must hold a posture that communicates:
- I am here for as long as you need
- I know this system completely
- I will not oversell
- I have no agenda beyond your understanding

Feet planted at The Arch. Weight balanced. One hand often extended slightly toward the approaching participant — not forcing, inviting. Head angled slightly to acknowledge the participant. When demonstrating the platform: a half-turn to indicate the demonstration, then back to direct engagement with the participant.

The posture is the posture of a good teacher who respects the student's skepticism.

### Motion Behavior
The Guide does not arrive — they are already there. Before Episode 1 begins, The Guide is simply present at The Arch, still, calm. When a participant approaches, The Guide turns to them — a slow, full-body turn, not just a head turn. Not reactive. Welcoming.

During dialogue: very few gestures. The words do the work. Occasional gestures toward The Arch itself when describing what it represents. Occasional gestures toward the participant when addressing their specific concern.

The Guide is the least animated character on screen. The stillness is the signal of trustworthiness.

### Environmental Cues
The Arch is the Guide's environment. The three tori surround them. The Council Ring strands await activation behind them. The Guide should feel like the custodian of this structure — at home within the geometry that represents the platform. The teal emissive from the inner torus reflects in the Guide's scene lighting subtly.

### Cultural References
This character should not be modeled on a Western archetype. Research:
- East African elders who hold community mediation roles — the pattern of authority through knowledge and calm, not through position
- The concept of the *mzee* — not elderly, but someone whose experience commands respect without requiring it
- Nairobi tech founders who communicate with community rather than at it — the Andela founder communication model; Safaricom social responsibility leadership tone
- The difference between a salesperson and an advisor — the advisor's stillness, the advisor's restraint from persuasion
- Research reference: Bitange Ndemo (former Kenya PS for ICT) communication style; early M-Pesa community education program facilitator documentation

The Guide should feel like they could exist in a Nairobi community hall, in a rural cooperative meeting, in a university forum — without being out of place in any of them.

### Recognition Test
The Guide must be recognizable as "the platform person" within 2 seconds — not because of a badge or logo, but because the posture and presence communicate stability and knowledge. A visitor seeing the scene must feel: that still figure at the center knows everything about this place.

### Failure Modes
- Any mascot quality (oversized eyes, simplified face, playful proportion) → destroys trustworthiness
- Corporate attire → becomes an executive, loses the steward quality
- Casual attire → loses the platform authority
- Too many accessories → the character becomes about objects, not presence
- Excessive animation → the restlessness reads as anxiety, not confidence
- Identical silhouette to any participant → loses the "central, constant presence" reading

## Pre-Modeling Checklist

Before committing any 3D geometry:

- [ ] Silhouette test passed (flat black, 2 seconds, correct role identified)
- [ ] Primary recognition cue visible from 45° angle (the viewing angle in the scene)
- [ ] Posture distinct from all adjacent roles
- [ ] Cultural references reviewed (no Western stock character defaults used)
- [ ] Clothing does not read as "costume" (authentic wear patterns considered)
- [ ] Administrator (Guide) silhouette remains distinct when all 6 participants are on screen simultaneously

## What This Document Is Not

This is not a character design brief for visual interest. It is not an art direction brief for aesthetic quality. It is a functional specification for instant recognition.

Every decision — every accessory, every posture choice, every clothing direction — must serve one question: will a first-time visitor understand who this person is before they read a single word?

If yes: proceed.
If no: redesign.
